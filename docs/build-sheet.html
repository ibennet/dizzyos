The design Sheet 1 — drawings

A shadow box: four pieces of 1×3, an acrylic face, and a plywood back that carries the panels and the Pi as one removable unit. No joinery beyond butt joints and screws, and Home Depot can make every wood cut for you.

Who cuts what. Every dimension here is a whole eighth of an inch, because that is about the tolerance a store panel saw holds — asking for sixteenths is asking for precision nobody can verify.

Let Home Depot cut the plywood. Breaking down a sheet by hand is awkward, and the back panel is hidden — a sixteenth out costs you nothing.

Cut the 1×3 yourself, with a $15 miter box. It holds the saw square for you, it is more accurate than the store's saw, and these are the cuts that decide whether your frame is square. What matters is that each pair matches — if both sides come out 7¹³⁄₁₆ the frame is still square and the panels still fit, but one at 7¾ and one at 7⅞ is a parallelogram forever. Cut one, then lay it on the board to mark the second rather than measuring again.

The acrylic is yours either way — they don't cut it. Score 6–8 passes against a straightedge, then snap it over a table edge.

Power — getting to one plug Sheet 2 — electrical

Adafruit's guidance is to keep the panel supply and the Pi supply separate — when the panels pull a current spike, a shared 5 V rail sags, and a Pi that browns out mid-write can corrupt the SD card. So: two supplies, one outlet.

Home Depot — Midtown East Sheet 3 — buy list

One trip covers everything, soldering included. Essentials first — skip nothing in that list — then tools you may already own, then the optional things with what each one actually gets you.

Rosin core, never acid core. The plumbing aisle sells acid-core solder and paste flux for copper pipe. Acid flux corrodes electronics and will eat the HAT over weeks. Buy rosin-core electronics solder from the tools/welding aisle. If the label says plumbing, water-soluble, or acid, put it back.

Assembly Sheet 5 — build order

Twenty-seven steps, written assuming you have never built anything. The model on the right shows where you are: the frame goes together first, the electronics get built up on the back board behind it, and that whole assembly seats into the case in one move near the end.

  1. Cut the 1×3 to length. Two pieces at 16¾ in, two at 7¾ in. Clamp the board in the miter box, saw in the vertical slot, and let the saw do the work — pushing hard makes it wander. Cut one piece of each pair, then lay it on the board to mark the second rather than measuring again: matching pairs matter far more than hitting the number exactly.
  2. Lay everything out. Put the four frame pieces, the back board and the acrylic on a flat surface — a floor is fine. Hold each pair side by side and check the ends line up. If a pair doesn't match, trim the longer one now; nothing later fixes it.
  3. Dry-fit the frame. "Dry-fit" means assemble it with no glue, just to see that it goes together. Stand the two short pieces upright and lay the long ones across the top and bottom, so the long pieces cap the ends of the short ones. That's the arrangement you'll glue.
  4. Check it's square. Measure corner to opposite corner, both ways. The two diagonals should be the same. If they differ, nudge the frame until they match — a rectangle with equal diagonals is square, and it's the only check you need.
  5. Sand now, not later. 120-grit first, then 220. Sanding a loose board is easy; sanding into the corners of a glued frame is miserable. Go with the grain — the stripes in the wood — not across it.
  6. Mark your screw holes. On each long piece, make two pencil marks near each end: about ½ in and 1½ in from the end, centered across the width. Eight marks total. These land in the end of the short pieces underneath.
  7. Drill pilot holes. A pilot hole is a small hole drilled before the screw. Use a bit slightly thinner than the screw's shaft. Skipping this is the classic beginner mistake: a screw driven into bare wood near an end acts like a wedge and splits the board. Drill through the long piece only, right through, at each mark.
  8. Glue and clamp the first corner. Thin bead of wood glue on the end of a short piece, press the long piece over it, line the outside edges up flush, and clamp. Squeeze-out — glue beading along the seam — means you used enough; wipe it with a damp cloth now, because dried glue won't take stain.
  9. Drive the screws. Two screws through the pilot holes into the piece beneath. Stop as soon as the head sits flush; a driver on full torque will strip the hole and then the screw just spins. Now unclamp — the screws hold it.
  10. Do the other three corners. Same sequence each time: glue, line up flush, clamp, screw. Work around the frame rather than doing two opposite corners first, so any small error has somewhere to go.
  11. Add the corner braces. Screw a flat L-bracket across each inside corner with the short screws. Butt joints — flat wood against flat wood — have almost no strength on their own and will rack into a parallelogram over time. The braces are what keep it a rectangle.
  12. Re-check square, then let the glue cure. Measure the diagonals again before the glue sets; you can still persuade it. Then leave it alone for an hour.
  13. Paint the inside of the opening matte black. This is the part people skip and regret. Any bare or light-colored wood inside the frame catches light from the LEDs and glows as a halo behind the acrylic. Black makes it disappear. Then finish the outside however you like and let it dry fully.
  14. Solder the two jumpers on the HAT — while it's loose. Two blobs: the middle pad to 8, and GPIO4 to GPIO18. Heat the pad with the iron for a second, feed solder into the joint (not onto the iron), and let it flow across. Do this now, on the bench, with the board in your hand — not later, bolted into a finished case. Check each with a multimeter on continuity: it beeps when connected.
  15. Mark and drill the back board. Lay the back board down, sit both panels on it face-down, positioned as they'll sit in the frame. Mark through the panels' mounting holes with a pencil, lift the panels off, and drill at each mark with a bit just big enough for an M3 screw to pass through.
  16. Fit the spacers. A spacer is a plain plastic tube that holds two things a fixed distance apart — no thread on it; the screw simply passes through the middle. Push an M3 screw up through each hole from the back, then slide a 1 in spacer and a ¾ in spacer over it, stacked end to end. That makes 1¾ in.

    Two parts rather than one because Home Depot's spacers stop at 1 in. The distance you actually want is 48.5 mm, and 1¾ in is 44.45 mm — about 4 mm short, which is the point. Stacking to 2 in instead would overshoot by 2 mm, standing the panels proud of the frame so the acrylic presses on them and bows. Short is free; long is a bowed face.
  17. Bolt on the first panel. Line its mounting holes up with the standoffs and screw it down. Snug, not cranked — you're threading into a small fitting and it will strip.
  18. Bolt on the second panel — push it tight against the first. This is the one place in the whole build with no tolerance. The pixels sit half a gap in from each panel's edge, so when the two panels touch, the grid continues across the seam perfectly. Leave even a couple of millimetres and you get a dark stripe down the middle of every image — and the sign draws text straight across it. Press the second panel firmly against the first, then tighten. Also sight along the faces from the side: they should be level with each other, no step.
  19. Mount the Pi. Four short M2.5 standoffs on the back board — a smaller thread than the panels use, so don't mix them up. Position it clear of the panel standoffs, and where you can still reach the SD card slot with a fingernail.
  20. Connect the ribbon cables. One from the HAT to panel 1's socket marked IN; a second from panel 1's OUT to panel 2's IN. The plugs are notched and only fit one way — if it resists, turn it over rather than pushing harder.
  21. Wire the power. The Y-cable's two plugs go into the panels' power sockets; its spade ends go into the HAT's screw terminals, red to +, black to −. Loosen the screw, insert the whole metal end, tighten, then tug it to confirm it's gripped. This is the one connection with no undo — check it twice.
  22. Test it on the bench, before it goes in. Plug in the panel supply first, then the Pi's. Wait 30 seconds; the sign should start on its own. Finding a bad ribbon now takes a minute. Finding it after the case is closed takes an evening.
  23. Drill the cord exit. Power off and unplug. A ½ in hole low in the back board, big enough for the power leads to pass. Sand the inside edge smooth so it can't chafe through the cable insulation.
  24. Cut the acrylic. Leave the protective film on. Mark your line, hold a straightedge along it, and drag the scoring tool 6–8 firm passes — you're cutting a groove, not slicing through. Then lay the score over a table edge and press down sharply; it snaps along the line. Ease the cut edge with 220-grit.
  25. Decide about frosting — with the sign lit. Power the panels up again and hold the clear acrylic over them. Bare LEDs behind clear acrylic look sharp but the black grid between the pixels reads as a grid, and over a mostly-dark display the sheet acts as a mirror. Frosting hides both, at a little brightness. Compare before committing: frost one of your offcuts and hold the two side by side over the running sign.

    To frost it, either apply window film — spray the acrylic with a little soapy water, lay the film on, squeegee from the middle outward, which is reversible if you hate it — or sand it with 400-grit wet paper, even circular pressure on one face until the sheen is uniformly matte, which is not. Try the matte face toward the LEDs and away from them; they look noticeably different.
  26. Seat the back assembly. Lift the whole back board — panels, Pi, wiring and all — and lower it into the frame from behind, feeding the power leads out through the hole. Screw it to the back of the frame with the short screws, every few inches around the edge.
  27. Fit the face and you're done. Peel the film off the acrylic and screw it to the front of the frame — go gently, acrylic cracks if you overtighten. Power up.
Never done this before? Two things worth knowing. Wood glue is stronger than the wood itself — the screws are really there to clamp the joint while it dries, so don't rely on screws alone and skip the glue. And nothing here is time-critical except glue-up: once glue is on a joint you have about ten minutes, so read a step through before starting it rather than halfway.
